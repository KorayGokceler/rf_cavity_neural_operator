\section{Discussion and conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Discussion}\label{sec:08-discussion}

\paragraph{A structural ceiling, now measured.}
By \cref{prop:upper-bound} every predicted $\theta_k$ lies above the discrete eigenvalue $\lambda_{h,k}$ of the space in which the model runs, so a trained model cannot be more accurate than the finite-element solution of that space.
The discrete $p{=}2$ eigenvalue on the model mesh lies at most \SI{0.047}{\percent} above the high-fidelity (HF) frequency on the six test geometries (\SI{0.033}{\percent} on four earlier ones). % src: docs/34 §8.1; docs/34 §4.1; EDITOR_NOTES_2 dec. 9
Because meshing and operator assembly are needed for every geometry, the model cannot undercut the cost of a solve in its own space by much, and the direct comparison with plain finite elements at these sizes (about $7\times10^3$ tetrahedra on average) is unfavourable to the model (\cref{sec:experiments}): % src: docs/35 §3b (6969 tets mean)
a first-order solve on the model mesh took \SI{1.7}{s} for a mean frequency error of \SI{1.25}{\percent}, a second-order solve on a mesh with 48\,\% fewer tetrahedra \SI{5.7}{s} for \SI{0.037}{\percent}, and the second-order solve on the model mesh (the LF label) \SI{13.9}{s} for \SI{0.019}{\percent}, against \SI{8.3}{s} and \SIrange{1.4}{1.7}{\percent} for the model path. % src: docs/35 §3b (single thread, idle machine); docs/34 §8.2 (8.3 s, one geometry, not re-measured)
At the sizes studied the model therefore has no speed or accuracy advantage over a direct solve, and the earlier comparison of \SI{8.3}{s} with \SI{18}{s} was not reproduced. % src: docs/35 §3b
The accuracy is also far from what final designs require ($10^{-5}$--$10^{-4}$), % src: docs/33 §6
so the model is at best a design-stage surrogate.

\paragraph{A parametric baseline on one family.}
On the single family studied, a Gaussian process on the six geometry parameters matched the model once 24 or more geometries were available: with 40 LF geometries it reached \SI{1.01}{\percent} mean and \SI{1.82}{\percent} maximum frequency error, against \SI{1.37}{\percent} and \SI{5.30}{\percent} for the model, and with 24 geometries \SI{1.64}{\percent} against \SI{1.55}{\percent}. % src: docs/35 §3a
With 8 geometries the model was about 2.3 times better (\SI{1.66}{\percent} against \SI{3.76}{\percent}), but its training labels were HF whereas the GP was equally accurate with LF and HF labels (\SI{3.75}{\percent} and \SI{3.76}{\percent}). % src: docs/35 §3a
The comparison has limits: one family with six parameters, six test geometries (two slightly outside the training box), single model runs, and a regressor that returns frequencies only and no fields. % src: docs/35 §3a (limitations)
It does show that at this scale the number of training geometries, rather than the label fidelity, governs the accuracy of both models.

\paragraph{What is guaranteed.}
The upper bound holds exactly in exact arithmetic, provided the kernel assumption (A) of \cref{sec:method} holds, which we verified numerically in all 15 cases tested (five curved meshes, $p=1,2,3$; small meshes only because the check needs dense linear algebra). % src: docs/35 §1
For the implemented conjugate-gradient projection the bound holds up to a relative perturbation $\bar\eta\approx(1\text{--}3)\,\tau_{\mathrm{CG}}$, about $10^{-8}$ at the shipped tolerance $\tau_{\mathrm{CG}}=10^{-8}$, and the a~posteriori form $\tilde\theta_k\ge\lambda_{h,k}/(1+\bar\eta)$ held in every run with $\bar\eta<1$. % src: docs/35 §2 (156 + 104 runs)
Violations of the plain bound occurred only for bases with a large gradient admixture and were at most $1.5\times10^{-8}$ (relative) at the shipped tolerance, many orders below the model error; a trained basis has a gradient fraction of about 0.5--0.6 before projection, for which the worst violations were $2\times10^{-11}$ and $1.4\times10^{-10}$ on two geometries. % src: docs/35 §2 (reading)
The drop rule for near-gradient directions protects only for $\tau_{\mathrm{CG}}\lesssim10^{-6}$: at $\tau_{\mathrm{CG}}=10^{-4}$ a pure-gradient basis gave $\bar\eta=1$ and $\theta\approx0$. % src: docs/35 §2
Without the projection, gradient-rich bases produce spurious modes (eight in the test, none with the projection at $\tau_{\mathrm{CG}}\le10^{-6}$), whereas random bases do not. % src: docs/35 §2 (v)
The bound is an index-wise bound on the discrete eigenvalues; it gives neither mode identity nor an accuracy bound for the exact eigenvalues.

\paragraph{Low-fidelity labels and the role of HF.}
Because the Ritz layer works in the model space, it can reach exactly the eigenvalues of the low-fidelity (LF) solve, which is why LF labels are natural for it (\cref{sec:multifidelity}).
Here the LF and HF labels differ by at most \SI{0.047}{\percent} in frequency and \SI{0.32}{\percent} in field, 30--300 times less than the model error, % src: docs/34 §8.1; referee §E
so the study does not probe the regime in which the label type matters; there the evidence is the 0.02-percentage-point LF$\to$HF gap and the label cost.
LF and HF do differ on wall-dominated quantities (measured on four geometries on earlier meshes; \cref{sec:multifidelity}), so HF labels remain necessary for any loss on such quantities and for test sets.

\paragraph{Where the approach could pay off (hypotheses).}
We have not shown an advantage over parametric regression on one low-dimensional family or over direct finite elements at about $10^4$ tetrahedra. The settings in which we expect it could are untested: geometry sets without a shared low-dimensional parametrisation (arbitrary CAD), where a regression on parameters is not available; predicted fields and figures of merit with a guaranteed eigenvalue ordering; warm starts for large finite-element solves (\cref{sec:08-future}); and larger meshes and GPUs, where the solve cost grows faster than the model's.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:08-limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scale and scope.} All runs are small and on a CPU: at most 40 training geometries, a 64-dimensional two-block trunk, 600 optimiser steps per arm, a single geometry family (pipe-terminated pillbox cavities) with a minimum fillet of 0.12, which rounds the geometry considerably, and six held-out geometries. % src: BRIEF §1; docs/34 §5, §8.2; EDITOR_NOTES_2 dec. 10
Absolute errors (\SIrange{1}{2}{\percent}) reflect an under-trained model; no learning curve was measured and generalisation across families is untested.
\item \textbf{The LF/HF comparison is confounded and weak.} Each arm of \cref{tab:equal-cost} is a single run without seed repeats, and fidelity and number of geometries change together (LF 8 against HF 8 was not run). We call the arms comparable; the robust results are the lower label cost and the small LF$\to$HF gap. % src: docs/34 §8.2; EDITOR_NOTES_2 dec. 2
\item \textbf{HF is one level.} The HF label is one third-order solve on the start mesh, so label and model mesh coincide; the adaptive refinement and the mesh split are not exercised by the training experiments. % src: docs/34 §3, §8.1; EDITOR_NOTES_2 dec. 3
\item \textbf{Baselines.} The GP and finite-element comparisons above use the model numbers of earlier single runs (no checkpoint was re-evaluated), the model time was measured on another geometry, and coarser curved meshes than 48\,\% fewer tetrahedra could not be generated. % src: docs/35 §3a, §3b (limitations)
\item \textbf{Checks cover small cases.} Assumption (A) was tested on meshes of up to about $6\times10^3$ free degrees of freedom, and the bound stress test on two geometries of one family with synthetic bases and one preconditioner. % src: docs/35 §1, §2 (limitations)
\item \textbf{Wall quantities are not trained.} The figures-of-merit loss is disabled for the field model. % src: docs/31 §2 (qoi_weight = 0)
\item \textbf{Inconclusive HF use.} Fine-tuning on two HF geometries gave no measurable gain over continued LF training; a later fine-tuned arm improved (\SI{1.37}{\percent} to \SI{1.13}{\percent}) but its control run was lost, and two mixed LF+HF runs failed. % src: docs/34 §4.3, §8.2
\item \textbf{Richer spaces do not help.} The same weights stay above the finite-element eigenvalue of the evaluated space in a cubic space and on a finer mesh, but the frequency error grows by 0.2 to 0.7 percentage points; a plausible, untested cause is the changed token density. % src: docs/34 §4.2; EDITOR_NOTES_2 dec. 6
\item \textbf{Deformed geometries and hardware.} Experiments use nominal geometries (on deformed meshes the label projection error is unmeasured), and the GPU path has not been run. % src: docs/31 §3.8, §3.2
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}\label{sec:08-future}

\paragraph{Hybrid inference.}
The predicted subspace can serve as the starting block of the LOBPCG eigensolver~\cite{knyazev2001toward} on the target space, so that accuracy is guaranteed by the solver.
For the cost side, our label solver gives a first indication at about $3.6\times10^5$ degrees of freedom (copper pillbox, third-order elements): a warm start from the previous adaptive level reached an eigenvalue error of $1.9\times10^{-8}$ in 10 iterations (\SI{36}{s}), and a cold start needed 40 iterations (\SI{123}{s}) for $1.7\times10^{-8}$. % src: docs/31 §3.4 (358k DOF)
Including the \SI{26}{s} set-up and the \SI{68}{s} transfer of the modes to the new mesh, the totals are about \SI{130}{s} (warm), \SI{149}{s} (cold) and \SI{175}{s} (direct solve), a marginal gain. % src: docs/31 §3.4: 36+26+68 = 130 s; 123+26 = 149 s; direct 175 s; EDITOR_NOTES_2 dec. 12
This is a property of the label solver, not a model result; whether a model subspace is a good start is untested.

\paragraph{Training, families and validation.}
Next steps are the missing LF-8 versus HF-8 control with several seeds, scaled LF+HF training with sweeps of the fidelity weights, a wall-quantity loss, and further families (quarter-wave resonators, drift-tube cells, RF-dipole cavities, photoinjector guns), where the comparison with parametric regression is the decisive test: a geometry set that no low-dimensional parametrisation covers. % src: docs/34 §6, §8.3; docs/33 §4, §8; docs/31 §5, §6; referee §5.1
A subset of each family should be cross-checked with an independent open-source finite-element code such as Palace~\cite{awslabs2023palace}, and GPU measurements are needed to settle which part of the training step dominates. % src: docs/31 §4, §3.6

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:08-conclusion}

The surrogate predicts a basis of fields and lets a conforming-space projection and a Rayleigh--Ritz step produce the eigenpairs; its frequencies are upper bounds of the discrete eigenvalues of the space it runs in, exactly in exact arithmetic and up to a relative error of order $\tau_{\mathrm{CG}}$, verified a~posteriori, for the implemented projection, and free of spurious gradient modes.
A validated generator supplies the labels, and a single second-order solve on the model mesh is a cheap substitute: it costs 2.8 to 4.3 times less than a third-order one (depending on the machine load) and differs from it by at most \SI{0.047}{\percent} in frequency. % src: docs/34 §8.1; EDITOR_NOTES_2 dec. 4
Models trained on such labels were comparable to a model trained on HF labels (\cref{tab:equal-cost}), with a LF$\to$HF gap of 0.02 percentage points; % src: docs/34 §8.2
in this small study the number of training geometries decided the accuracy more than the label fidelity, for the model and for a parametric regression.
We did not show an accuracy or speed advantage over such a regression on one low-dimensional family or over direct finite elements at these mesh sizes. The contribution is the construction with its discrete guarantee, the data pipeline, and controlled small-scale evidence; whether the approach pays off on arbitrary CAD geometries, at larger sizes or with warm starts remains to be tested.
