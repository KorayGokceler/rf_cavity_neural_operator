\section{Discussion and conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Discussion}\label{sec:08-discussion}

\paragraph{A structural ceiling.}
By \cref{prop:upper-bound} every predicted $\theta_k$ lies above the discrete eigenvalue $\lambda_{h,k}$ of the space in which the model runs, so a trained model cannot be more accurate than the finite-element solution of that space.
On the four geometries where we measured it, the second-order eigenvalue on the model mesh lies within \SI{0.033}{\percent} of the high-fidelity (HF) frequency. % src: docs/34 §4.1 (FE floor <= 0.033 %)
This is small next to the \SIrange{1}{2}{\percent} error of our models but not next to the $10^{-5}$--$10^{-4}$ accuracy that final cavity designs require, % src: docs/33 §6
so the model is a design-stage surrogate and not a replacement for a production solver.
For parameter sweeps a frequency offset can be absorbed by rescaling or tuning, and the figures of merit matter more. % src: docs/33 §6

\paragraph{Where the speed comes from.}
Meshing and operator assembly are needed for every geometry and stay in the inference path, so at a fixed discretisation the gain over a direct solve is bounded to a small factor.
On a single thread, assembling the operators took \SI{5}{s} and the forward pass \SI{3.3}{s}, against \SI{18}{s} for the finite-element solve in the same space; % src: docs/34 §8.2 (inference time)
at the smallest sizes tested the two costs were of the same order, and the model path was about 2.5 times faster in a cubic space and about 3.5 times faster on an eight-fold finer mesh (two threads, CPU). % src: docs/34 §4.2
We therefore claim no large per-query speed-up. The expected benefits are amortised design-space exploration, warm starts for an iterative solver (\cref{sec:08-future}), the guarantees (upper bound, no spurious zero modes, valid for any weights), and cheaper training data. We have not quantified which of these pays off in practice.

\paragraph{Low-fidelity training and what HF is for.}
Because the Ritz layer works in the model space, it can reach exactly the eigenvalues of the low-fidelity (LF) solve, which is why LF labels suit it (\cref{sec:multifidelity}).
LF labels do not agree with HF labels on wall-dominated quantities (quality factor, peak fields), measured on four geometries on earlier, partly non-matching meshes (\cref{sec:multifidelity}). % src: docs/34 §3; EDITOR_NOTES 3
HF labels therefore remain necessary for any loss on such quantities and for test sets, whose reference must not share the model's discretisation error.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:08-limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scale and scope.} All runs are small and on a CPU: at most 40 training geometries, a 64-dimensional two-block trunk, 600 optimiser steps per arm in the equal-cost study, a single geometry family (pipe-terminated pillbox cavities with a lower bound on the fillet radius) and six held-out geometries. % src: BRIEF §1; docs/34 §5, §8.2
Absolute errors (about \SIrange{1}{2}{\percent}) reflect an under-trained model, no learning curve was measured, and generalisation across families and topologies is untested.
\item \textbf{Single runs.} Each arm of \cref{tab:equal-cost} is one training run, so small differences between arms are not statistically established; the 0.02-percentage-point LF$\to$HF gap is the more robust result. % src: docs/34 §8.2; EDITOR_NOTES 1
\item \textbf{Wall quantities are not trained.} The figures-of-merit loss is disabled for the field model. % src: docs/31 §2 (qoi_weight = 0)
\item \textbf{Inconclusive HF use.} Fine-tuning on two HF geometries gave no measurable gain over continued LF training. In the equal-cost study the fine-tuned arm improved on its parent (\SI{1.37}{\percent} to \SI{1.13}{\percent}), but its control run was lost, so the cause cannot be attributed; two mixed LF+HF runs failed (a schedule on which the learning rate never decayed, and a lost run). % src: docs/34 §4.3, §8.2
\item \textbf{Richer spaces do not help.} The same weights keep the upper bound in a cubic space or on a finer mesh, but the frequency error grows by 0.2 to 0.6 percentage points. % src: docs/34 §4.2
\item \textbf{Deformed geometries and hardware.} All experiments use nominal geometries; for deformed meshes about \SI{11}{\percent} of points were not found by point location and the label projection error is unmeasured. % src: docs/31 §3.8
Only CPU timings exist; the GPU path has not been run. % src: docs/31 §3.2, §3.6
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}\label{sec:08-future}

\paragraph{Hybrid inference.}
The predicted subspace can start a preconditioned eigensolver such as LOBPCG~\cite{knyazev2001toward} on the target space, so that accuracy is guaranteed by the solver and speed by the model.
In a preliminary measurement with our label solver at about $3.6\times10^5$ degrees of freedom, a warm start from the previous adaptive level reached an eigenvalue error of $1.9\times10^{-8}$ in 10 iterations (\SI{36}{s}), whereas a cold start needed 40 iterations (\SI{123}{s}) for $1.7\times10^{-8}$; the direct solve took \SI{175}{s}, and the warm-start time excludes a \SI{26}{s} set-up and the mode transfer. % src: docs/31 §3.4 (358k DOF)
Whether a model subspace is as good a start is untested; we expect a gain only when the solve takes minutes and the model's initial error is at most about \SI{0.1}{\percent}. % src: docs/34 §6

\paragraph{Training, families and validation.}
Next steps are scaled LF+HF training (hundreds of LF and tens of HF geometries) with sweeps of the fidelity weights and a proper control for fine-tuning, a wall-quantity loss, and further families (quarter-wave resonators, drift-tube cells, RF-dipole cavities, photoinjector guns) with a validation set of published designs. % src: docs/34 §6, §8.3; docs/33 §4, §8; docs/31 §5, §6
A subset of each family should be cross-checked with an independent open-source finite-element code such as Palace~\cite{palace2023software}, and GPU measurements are needed to settle which part of the training step dominates. % src: docs/31 §4, §3.6

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:08-conclusion}

Predicting a basis of fields and letting a Nédélec-space projection and a Rayleigh--Ritz step produce the eigenpairs gives a surrogate whose frequencies are upper bounds of the discrete eigenvalues of the space it runs in and free of spurious gradient modes, with eigenvalue errors of $1\times10^{-6}$ to $9\times10^{-6}$ in single-sample overfitting against $2.4\times10^{-3}$ for a lowest-order model. % src: docs/28 §3
Since the Ritz layer targets the eigenvalues of its own space, a single second-order solve on the model mesh is a sufficient label: it costs 4.3 times less than an adaptive third-order one and differs from it by at most \SI{0.047}{\percent} in frequency. % src: docs/34 §8.1
In our equal-cost comparison (\cref{tab:equal-cost}), LF training at equal or lower label cost was at least as accurate on six held-out HF geometries as HF training, with an LF$\to$HF gap of 0.02 percentage points, % src: docs/34 §8.2
but these are single runs on one family at small scale and should be read as controlled evidence for the method and data pipeline, not as production-grade accuracy.
