\section{Discussion and conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Discussion}\label{sec:08-discussion}

\paragraph{A structural ceiling.}
The Rayleigh--Ritz layer returns the best approximation of the discrete eigenpairs that the span of the learned basis allows, and no better than the discrete problem itself: by \cref{prop:upper-bound} every predicted $\theta_k$ lies above $\lambda_{h,k}$ of the space in which the model runs.
A trained model therefore cannot be more accurate than the finite-element solution of its own space.
On the model meshes used here the frequency floor of the $p{=}2$ solution is \SIrange{0.04}{0.9}{\percent}, % src: docs/31 §1 (docs/30 §5)
which is far coarser than the $10^{-5}$--$10^{-4}$ accuracy that final cavity designs require. % src: docs/33 §6
The model is thus a design-stage surrogate and not a replacement for a production solver.
For parameter sweeps the frequency offset can be absorbed by rescaling or tuning, and the figures of merit matter more; for final tuning the ceiling is binding. % src: docs/33 §6

\paragraph{Where the speed comes from.}
Meshing and operator assembly are needed per geometry and remain in the inference path, so at a fixed discretisation the gain over a direct solve is bounded to a small factor.
The measurements agree: on a single thread, assembling the operators took \SI{5}{s} and the forward pass \SI{3.3}{s}, against \SI{18}{s} for the finite-element solve in the same space; % src: docs/34 §8.2 (inference time)
at the smallest sizes tested the two costs were of the same order, and the model path was about 2.5 times faster in a cubic space and about 3.5 times faster on an eight-fold finer mesh (two threads, CPU). % src: docs/34 §4.2
We therefore do not claim a large per-query speed-up. The expected benefit lies elsewhere:
(i)~amortised design-space exploration, where the per-geometry cost is that of the operators and one forward pass;
(ii)~warm starts, since the predicted subspace is a candidate starting block for an iterative solver (\cref{sec:08-future});
(iii)~the guarantees, namely the upper-bound property and the absence of spurious zero modes, which hold for any weights, trained or not; and
(iv)~cheaper training data, as discussed next.
We have not quantified which of these pays off in practice.

\paragraph{Why low-fidelity training works here.}
Because the Ritz layer assembles $\Kmat$, $\Mmat$ and $\Gmat$ in the model space, the eigenvalues it can reach exactly are those of that space, which are the eigenvalues of the low-fidelity (LF) solve.
On the shared start mesh the LF and high-fidelity (HF) labels differ by \SIrange{0.004}{0.047}{\percent} in frequency and by \SIrange{0.08}{0.32}{\percent} in the $\Mmat$-norm field-subspace distance, % src: docs/34 §8.1
a difference one to two orders of magnitude below the error of the trained model, % src: docs/34 §8.1 ("model error 10–50x")
while an LF label costs 4.3 times less. % src: docs/34 §8.1
At a given labelling budget this buys more training geometries, and in our comparison it gave a lower held-out error (\cref{tab:equal-cost}). % src: docs/34 §8.2
This holds on the family studied, where frequency and volume field are nearly identical at both fidelities.

\paragraph{What HF is still for.}
LF and HF labels do not agree on wall-dominated quantities.
On four geometries the LF quality factor was \SIrange{0.8}{1.4}{\percent} lower, the peak electric field \SIrange{4}{28}{\percent} lower and the peak magnetic field \SIrange{5}{8}{\percent} higher than the HF value. % src: docs/31 §3.9 (docs/34 §3), LF on its own coarser mesh
HF labels are thus indispensable for any loss that targets such quantities, and for test sets and cross-checks, where the reference must not share the model's discretisation error.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:08-limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scale.} All training runs are small and on a CPU: at most 40 training geometries, a model with a 64-dimensional, two-block trunk, and 600 optimiser steps per arm in the equal-cost experiment. % src: BRIEF §1; docs/34 §8.2 (600 steps), §4 model
The absolute errors (about \SIrange{1}{2}{\percent} in frequency) reflect an under-trained model and say nothing about the ceiling of the method; no learning curve was measured.
\item \textbf{One geometry family.} Every experiment uses one family (pipe-terminated pillbox cavities), with a fixed lower bound on the fillet radius and a held-out set of six geometries. % src: docs/34 §5, §8.2
Generalisation across families and topologies is untested. Quarter-wave resonators, drift-tube cells and RF guns are not yet in the data. % src: docs/33 §4, docs/31 §6 item 10
\item \textbf{Wall quantities are not trained.} The figures-of-merit loss is disabled for the field model, so no wall-dominated quantity is learned. % src: docs/31 §2 (qoi_weight = 0), docs/34 §4.3
\item \textbf{Inconclusive HF use.} Fine-tuning on two HF geometries gave no measurable gain over continued LF training at the same epoch count, and in the equal-cost study the fine-tuned arm improved on its parent (\SI{1.37}{\percent} to \SI{1.13}{\percent}) while the matching control run was lost with the machine state, so the cause cannot be attributed. % src: docs/34 §4.3, §8.2
Two mixed LF+HF runs failed (a schedule on which the learning rate never decayed, and a lost run), so mixed training is untested. % src: docs/34 §8.2
\item \textbf{Deformed geometries.} All experiments use nominal (undeformed) geometries. For deformed meshes, a point-location test failed for about 11\,\% of points under the deformation, and the resulting projection error of the labels has not been measured. % src: docs/31 §3.8 (open item 3)
\item \textbf{Hardware.} Only CPU timings exist; the GPU path has not been run or measured, so the training-step profile, in which the trunk dominates on the CPU, may change substantially. % src: docs/31 §3.2, §3.6
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}\label{sec:08-future}

\paragraph{Hybrid inference.}
The predicted subspace can serve as the starting block of a preconditioned iterative eigensolver such as LOBPCG~\cite{knyazev2001toward} on the target space, so that accuracy is guaranteed by the solver and speed by the model.
In a preliminary solver measurement on a cavity with about $3.6\times10^5$ degrees of freedom, a warm start from the previous adaptive level reached an eigenvalue error of $1.9\times10^{-8}$ in 10 iterations (\SI{36}{s}) while a cold start needed 40 iterations (\SI{123}{s}) for $1.7\times10^{-8}$; the direct solve took \SI{175}{s}. The warm-start time excludes a set-up cost of \SI{26}{s} and the transfer of the modes to the new mesh. % src: docs/31 §3.4 (358k DOF)
Whether a model subspace is as good a start as a previous refinement level is untested.
We expect a gain only when the solver takes minutes and the initial model error is at most about \SI{0.1}{\percent}, which the present model does not reach. % src: docs/34 §6

\paragraph{Training at scale.}
The next experiments are scaled LF+HF training with several hundred LF and a few tens of HF geometries, sweeps of the fidelity weight and frequency scale, and a correct control for the fine-tuning question. % src: docs/34 §6, §8.3
A wall-quantity loss is a prerequisite for HF labels to add information, and a pilot on one or two families should precede large-scale label production. % src: docs/31 §5, §6 item 1; docs/34 §6

\paragraph{Families, validation and hardware.}
Priority families are quarter-wave resonators, drift-tube cells, ridged (RF-dipole) cavities and photoinjector guns, with a validation set built from published designs and tighter label tolerances for test than for training sets. % src: docs/33 §4, §8; docs/31 §5
A subset of each family should be cross-checked with an independent open-source finite-element code such as Palace~\cite{palace2023software}, and GPU measurements are needed to settle which part of the training step dominates. % src: docs/31 §4, §3.6 (open item 9)

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:08-conclusion}

We proposed a neural operator for cavity eigenmodes in which the network predicts a basis of vector fields that a fixed Nédélec-element map, a float64 kernel projection and a Rayleigh--Ritz step turn into frequencies and fields.
The predicted eigenvalues are upper bounds of the discrete eigenvalues of the space in which the model runs, and spurious gradient modes are removed by construction.
Quadrature-point tokens reached relative eigenvalue errors of $1\times10^{-6}$ to $9\times10^{-6}$ in single-sample overfitting, against $2.4\times10^{-3}$ for a lowest-order model. % src: docs/28 §3; BRIEF §1 item 3
An adaptive third-order label generator on curved meshes, validated against analytic pillbox solutions, supplies the HF labels.
A single second-order solve on the model mesh yields LF labels at $1/4.3$ of the cost that differ from them by \SIrange{0.004}{0.047}{\percent} in frequency and \SIrange{0.08}{0.32}{\percent} in field, % src: docs/34 §8.1
and models trained on such labels were more accurate on six held-out HF geometries than a model trained on HF labels: mean frequency error \SI{1.55}{\percent} with an LF arm of 20\,\% lower labelling cost and \SI{1.37}{\percent} with one of 1.4 times the cost, against \SI{1.66}{\percent} for HF labels, with an LF-to-HF gap of 0.02 percentage points. % src: docs/34 §8.2 (arms B2, C2, A)
These results come from small-scale runs on one geometry family and should be read as controlled evidence for the method and data pipeline, not as a demonstration of production-grade accuracy; the open points above indicate what is needed to reach that.
