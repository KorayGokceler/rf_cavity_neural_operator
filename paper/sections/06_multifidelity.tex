\section{Low-fidelity labels and multi-fidelity training}\label{sec:multifidelity}

Multi-fidelity learning combines a few expensive, accurate samples with many cheap, approximate ones
\citep{kennedy2000predicting,peherstorfer2018survey}; for neural surrogates this is usually realised by composite
networks that correct a low-fidelity prediction \citep{meng2020composite,lu2022multifidelity,howard2023multifidelity}.
Here the two fidelities are two \emph{label generators} for the same model; experiments are in \cref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{Two label fidelities}\label{sec:06-defs}

\paragraph{High fidelity ($\HF$).} The label is the order-3 solution of the adaptive generator of \cref{sec:data}.
Frequencies and figures of merit are taken from the order-3 label mesh; the training field target is the
$M$-orthogonal projection of the order-3 modes onto the order-2 space $\Vh$ of the model mesh. In every experiment of
this paper the element budget (at most 150\,000 label DOFs) is exhausted by the first order-3 solve, so the $\HF$
label is \emph{one} order-3 solve on the start mesh and the label mesh equals the model mesh; the adaptive loop and
the label/model-mesh split are part of the generator but are not exercised by the training experiments.
% src: docs/34 §3 (adapt_iters = 1, adapt_stop = budget), §8.1; EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 3

\paragraph{Low fidelity ($\LF$).} The label is a single order-2 solve directly on the model mesh: no adaptivity and no
transfer between meshes (a solve computes six modes; the experiments predict $K=4$). Because the label already lies
in the model space, the field target carries no projection error (the projection residual of the stored label is of
order $10^{-7}$).
% src: docs/34 §1 (proj_err ~1e-7); docs/34 §8.1 (6 modes); EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 11

\paragraph{Why $\LF$ labels suit this model.} The Ritz layer computes eigenpairs of the model space itself, so the
frequencies it can attain are bounded below by the discrete order-2 eigenvalues $\lamh$ of that space
(\cref{prop:upper-bound}); these eigenvalues are the floor of the model space. $\LF$ frequencies are exactly these
eigenvalues, so the bound is tight at the label and a perfect basis reproduces it. An $\HF$ frequency is closer to
the exact eigenvalue and lies below the floor: the discrete order-2 eigenvalue on the model mesh lies at most
$0.047\,\%$ above the $\HF$ frequency on the six test geometries ($0.033\,\%$ on four earlier geometries); this is
the $\LF$--$\HF$ difference of \cref{sec:06-diff}, positive in every measured case.
% src: docs/34 §8.1 (+0.004–+0.047 %), §4.1 table (FE floor ≤ 0.021/0.033/0.009/0.017 %); EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 9
The $\HF$ field target is a different matter: it is the projection of the order-3 modes onto the model space,
and the Rayleigh quotients of these projected targets exceed the order-3 frequencies by $0.04$--$0.9\,\%$ (two
pillbox-with-pipes model meshes of 11\,300 and 13\,052 tetrahedra), an upper bound of the floor and a measure of the
quality of the projected target rather than of the floor itself.
% src: docs/30 §5 "Model-space frequency floor" (2.4e-3…8.6e-3, 3.7e-4…); EDITOR_NOTES 2

\subsection{The shared start mesh}\label{sec:06-mesh}

Mixing the two fidelities is meaningful only on the same mesh. The adaptive path starts from the curved
mesh with the first of a list of curvature-safety settings (the coarser $1.5$ first, the default $2.0$ as a fallback
when elements fold) and does not retry a finer setting after a mesh that exceeds the element limit; the $\LF$ path
uses the same rule.
% src: src/data_gen/field_labels.py start_mesh; docs/34 §8.1
Our first $\LF$ generator did not, and produced meshes up to $1.9$ times larger than the $\HF$ model meshes; an earlier
observation that the $\LF$ mesh ``does not shrink'' was a misdiagnosis caused by this mismatch (\cref{sec:A06-meshes}).
% src: docs/34 §8.1 (1.2–1.9×), §3 (corrected in §8.1)
With the shared rule the meshes are identical to the $\HF$ model meshes on 8 of 8 training and 6 of 6 test geometries,
and the mean size over 40 $\LF$ samples drops from 9404 to 7639 tetrahedra ($-19\,\%$).
The label-time ratio $\HF$/$\LF$ is then between $2.8$ and $4.3$, depending on the measurement conditions
(\cref{tab:A06-cost}): $4.3$ on the eight training meshes (\SI{1242}{s} against \SI{291}{s}) under concurrent training
load, $2.8$--$3.7$ on two geometries without load; it was $2.3$ with the earlier $\LF$ meshes. Because the $\HF$
label is a single order-3 solve, this is the cost of an order-3 against an order-2 solve on one mesh, not a comparison
with a multi-level adaptive generator.
% src: docs/34 §8.1 (8/8, 6/6, 9404→7639 −19 %, 1242 s/291 s = 4.3, 2.8–3.7 unloaded, old ratio 2.3); EDITOR_NOTES_2 decisions 3–4

\subsection{How the labels differ}\label{sec:06-diff}

On identical meshes, the $\LF$ frequency exceeds the $\HF$ one by $0.004$--$0.047\,\%$, and the part of an $\HF$
mode that falls outside the span of the six $\LF$ modes has an $M$-norm of $0.08$--$0.32\,\%$ for modes 0--3. The
model's own errors (about $1$--$2\,\%$ in frequency and $4.5$--$5\,\%$ in the field, \cref{sec:experiments}) exceed
these differences by one to two orders of magnitude, so the experiments of this paper cannot yet discriminate between
the two label types; they do not probe the accuracy range in which the label type should matter.
% src: docs/34 §8.1 (freq +0.004–+0.047 %; field 0.08–0.32 %; model error 10–50× larger), §8.2 (1.1–1.7 %, 0.045–0.050); EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 2
Quantities determined by the wall behave differently (\cref{tab:06-diff}). These values were measured on the earlier
meshes, where the $\LF$ and $\HF$ meshes coincided for only one of the four geometries, so they mix the effect of the
fidelity with that of the mesh. They also contain the discretisation error of the $\HF$ reference, itself a single
order-3 solve on a coarse mesh (the order-3 $\Bpk$ of a pipe pillbox changed by $4\,\%$ between its two finest
adaptive levels, \cref{sec:data}). They were not repeated on identical meshes.

\subsection{Training mechanics}\label{sec:06-train}

Each sample carries a fidelity flag derived from its file metadata: a label produced with adaptivity, or with a label
order above the model order, is $\HF$; a single solve of the model order is $\LF$. Files of both kinds can be mixed.
% src: src/data/field_dataset.py label_fidelity
The span and frequency terms of the loss of \cref{sec:method} are weighted per sample,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}\omega_{\varphi_b}\Bigl(\alpha_{\mathrm{span}}\,\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{span}}_b
   +\alpha_{\mathrm{freq}}\,\beta_{\varphi_b}\,\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{freq}}_b\Bigr)
   +\alpha_{\mathrm{ss}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ss}}+\alpha_{\mathrm{ortho}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ortho}},
  \qquad \varphi_b\in\{\LF,\HF\},
\end{equation}
% src: src/training/lightning_module.py _span_terms, _compute_loss_3d (fidelity_weights → ω on span and frequency terms only; fidelity_freq_scale → β on the frequency term; selfsup and ortho unweighted)
with fidelity weights $\omega_\LF,\omega_\HF$ and an extra scale $\beta_\LF,\beta_\HF$ on the frequency term alone, since
$\HF$ frequencies lie below the floor of the model space; all default to one (no weighting). Per-fidelity errors are
logged separately.
% src: docs/34 §2
Three further mechanisms serve a scarce $\HF$ set: extra $\HF$ files that always enter the training split and never the
validation or test split; repetition of the $\HF$ geometries of the training split a given number of times per epoch; and
initialisation from the weights of a checkpoint, with fresh optimiser state and epoch counter, for fine-tuning an
$\LF$-trained model. A sweep of the fidelity scales and of the repetition count was not carried out, and the benefit of
mixed or fine-tuned training was not established at this scale (\cref{sec:experiments,sec:discussion}).
% src: docs/34 §2 (train_extra, hf_repeat, --init_from, frequency normalisation); §4.3, §6, §8.2 (sweep open; mixed and fine-tune runs inconclusive)
