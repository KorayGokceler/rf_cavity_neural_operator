\section{Low-fidelity labels and multi-fidelity training}\label{sec:multifidelity}

Multi-fidelity learning combines a few expensive, accurate samples with many cheap, approximate ones
\citep{kennedy2000predicting,peherstorfer2018survey}; for neural surrogates this is usually realised by
composite networks that correct a low-fidelity prediction with high-fidelity data
\citep{meng2020composite,lu2022multifidelity,howard2023multifidelity}. Here the two fidelities are two
\emph{label generators} for the same model, and the Ritz layer of \cref{sec:method} makes the cheap one natural.
Training experiments are in \cref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{Two label fidelities}\label{sec:06-defs}

\paragraph{High fidelity ($\HF$).} The label is the adaptive order-3 solution of \cref{sec:data}. Frequencies and
figures of merit are taken from the order-3 label mesh; the training field target is the $M$-orthogonal
projection of the order-3 modes onto the order-2 space $\Vh$ of the model mesh.

\paragraph{Low fidelity ($\LF$).} The label is a single order-2 solve directly on the model mesh: no adaptivity and
no transfer between meshes. Because the label already lies in the model space, the field target carries no
projection error (the projection residual of the stored label is of order $10^{-7}$;
% src: docs/34 §1 (proj_err ~1e-7)
the $\HF$ projection residual, in contrast, is $0.10$--$0.42\,\%$ on the four geometries of \cref{tab:06-diff}).
% src: docs/34 §3 table (HF proj_err 0.10–0.42 %)

\paragraph{Why $\LF$ labels suit this model.} The Ritz layer computes eigenpairs of the model space itself, so the
frequencies it can attain are bounded below by the discrete order-2 eigenvalues $\lamh$ of that space
(\cref{prop:upper-bound}); these eigenvalues are the floor of the model space. $\LF$ frequencies are exactly these
eigenvalues, so the bound is tight at the label and a perfect basis reproduces it. An $\HF$ frequency is closer to
the exact eigenvalue and lies below the floor: the order-2 eigenvalue exceeds it by at most $0.033\,\%$ on the four
geometries of the first transfer study, and the $\LF$ frequency exceeds the $\HF$ one by $0.004$--$0.047\,\%$ on
identical meshes (positive in every measured case).
% src: docs/34 §4.1 table (FE floor ≤ 0.021/0.033/0.009/0.017 %); docs/34 §8.1 (+0.004–+0.047 %)
The $\HF$ field target is a different matter: it is the projection of the order-3 modes onto the model space,
and the Rayleigh quotients of these projected targets exceed the order-3 frequencies by $0.04$--$0.9\,\%$ (two
pillbox-with-pipes model meshes of 11\,300 and 13\,052 tetrahedra), an upper bound of the floor and a measure of the
quality of the projected target rather than of the floor itself.
% src: docs/30 §5 "Model-space frequency floor" (2.4e-3…8.6e-3, 3.7e-4…); EDITOR_NOTES 2

\subsection{The shared start mesh}\label{sec:06-mesh}

Comparing or mixing the two fidelities is only meaningful on the same mesh. The adaptive path starts from the curved
mesh with the first of a list of curvature-safety settings (coarser first, the default $2.0$ as a fallback when
elements fold; $1.5$ is the coarser setting), and does not retry a finer setting after a mesh that exceeds the
element limit.
% src: src/data_gen/field_labels.py start_mesh; docs/34 §8.1
The first version of our $\LF$ generator used the finer setting only. On six test geometries this gave meshes
$1.2$--$1.9$ times larger than the $\HF$ model meshes ($5.9$k--$12.1$k against $4.8$k--$10.0$k tetrahedra), so the
cheap labels were more expensive than necessary and the mesh distribution at training differed from that at
inference. Switching to the coarser setting alone did not suffice, because on geometries where it folds elements
the $\LF$ path re-meshed more finely whereas the adaptive path fell back to the default.
% src: docs/34 §8.1 (1.2–1.9×, 5.9k–12.1k vs 4.8k–10.0k; cs 1.5 alone insufficient)
An earlier measurement had concluded that the $\LF$ mesh does not shrink whichever meshing parameter is varied;
this was a misdiagnosis caused by the mismatched start rule, and a pitfall worth recording.
% src: docs/34 §3 (corrected in §8.1)
Reusing the start-mesh rule gave meshes identical to the $\HF$ model meshes on 8 of 8 training and 6 of 6 test
geometries, reduced the mean size over 40 $\LF$ samples from 9404 to 7639 tetrahedra ($-19\,\%$), and raised the
cost ratio $\HF$/$\LF$ from $2.3$ to $4.3$ (\SI{1242}{s} against \SI{291}{s} on the same eight meshes; two workers
with one thread each on a 4-core machine under concurrent training load; $4.1$ on the six test geometries,
\SI{779}{s} against \SI{190}{s}).
% src: docs/34 §8.1 (8/8, 6/6, 9404→7639 −19 %, 1242 s/291 s = 4.3, old ratio 2.3, 779/190 s)
With the budget used here (at most 150\,000 label DOFs) the first order-3 solve exhausts the budget, so the
``adaptive'' $\HF$ label is a single order-3 solve on the start mesh; with a larger budget the $\HF$ model mesh may
be a refined level and the meshes would differ.
% src: docs/34 §8.1, §3, §5

\subsection{How the labels differ}\label{sec:06-diff}

On identical meshes, the $\LF$ frequency exceeds the $\HF$ one by $0.004$--$0.047\,\%$, and the part of an $\HF$
mode that falls outside the span of the six $\LF$ modes has an $M$-norm of $0.08$--$0.32\,\%$ for modes 0--3; the
model's own error is $10$--$50$ times larger than these differences.
% src: docs/34 §8.1 (freq +0.004–+0.047 %; field 0.08–0.32 %; 10–50×)
Quantities determined by the wall behave differently. \Cref{tab:06-diff} gives the differences for mode 0 of four
geometries, measured on the earlier meshes, where the $\LF$ and $\HF$ meshes coincided for only one of the four geometries; the

The conclusion is the expected one. Frequencies, volume fields and \RoQ{} are matched by $\LF$ labels to well
below the model error, whereas $\Qz$, the corresponding wall-loss quantities and the surface peaks are not, and
need $\HF$ labels. Since the quantity-of-interest loss is disabled for the field model in this study, the model
is not trained on the wall-dominated quantities and the experiments of \cref{sec:experiments} do not test
them.

\subsection{Training mechanics}\label{sec:06-train}

Each sample carries a fidelity flag derived from the metadata of its file: a label produced with adaptivity, or
with a label order above the model order, is $\HF$; a single solve of the model order is $\LF$. Files of both kinds can
therefore be mixed freely.
% src: src/data/field_dataset.py label_fidelity
The span and frequency terms of the loss of \cref{sec:method} are weighted per sample,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}\Bigl(w_{\varphi_b}\,\ell^{\mathrm{span}}_b
   +\lambda_f\,w_{\varphi_b}\,s_{\varphi_b}\,\ell^{\mathrm{freq}}_b\Bigr)+\cdots,
  \qquad \varphi_b\in\{\LF,\HF\},
\end{equation}
% src: src/training/lightning_module.py (fidelity_weights → w, fidelity_freq_scale → s; w_freq = w_fid * scale)
with fidelity weights $w_\LF,w_\HF$ and an extra scale $s_\LF,s_\HF$ on the frequency term alone, the latter because
$\HF$ frequencies lie below the floor of the model space; the other terms are unchanged. All weights default to one,
which reproduces training without fidelity weighting. When a batch contains both fidelities, frequency and field
errors are logged separately per fidelity.
% src: docs/34 §2
Three further mechanisms serve a scarce $\HF$ set. Additional files of $\HF$ samples can be supplied that always
enter the training split and never the validation or test split; the frequency normalisation is then computed from
the training split (or from these files if the split is empty). $\HF$ geometries of the training split can be
repeated a given number of times per epoch. Finally, training can be initialised from the weights of a
checkpoint, with a fresh optimiser state and epoch counter, for fine-tuning an $\LF$-trained model on $\HF$ samples.
% src: docs/34 §2 (train_extra, hf_repeat, --init_from, frequency normalisation)
A sweep of the fidelity scales and of the repetition count was not carried out, and the benefit of mixed or
fine-tuned training was not established at the scale of this study (\cref{sec:experiments,sec:discussion}).
