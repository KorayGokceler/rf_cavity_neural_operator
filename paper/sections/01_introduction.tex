\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

The resonant modes of a radio-frequency (RF) cavity determine its use in a particle accelerator.
For a superconducting cavity such as the nine-cell \SI{1.3}{GHz} niobium structure of the TESLA design \citep{aune2000superconducting,padamsee2009rf},
the designer needs not only the mode frequencies but also the fields and the derived figures of merit:
the unloaded quality factor $\Qz$, the geometry factor, the shunt impedance $\RoQ$, the transit-time factor, and the
peak-field ratios $\Epk/\Eacc$ and $\Bpk/\Eacc$ that limit the achievable gradient.
Each design iteration changes the shape and needs a new three-dimensional eigenvalue solve on a curved geometry.
Axisymmetric codes \citep{halbach1976superfish} are cheap but cover only part of the design space;
general three-dimensional design relies on finite-element eigensolvers such as the ACE3P suite \citep{ko2010advances} or commercial tools such as CST Studio Suite,
whose cost per geometry grows with the mesh resolution needed for accurate figures of merit.
A surrogate that returns frequencies, fields and figures of merit directly from the geometry would shorten the loop,
provided it can be trusted at the accuracy of the solver it replaces.

Neural operators \citep{lu2021learning,li2021fourier,kovachki2023neural} are natural candidates, since they learn the map from
a PDE's input (here the geometry) to its solution.
Applying them directly to the Maxwell eigenproblem $\curl\curl\,\E=k^2\E$ in $\Omega$ with $\mathbf{n}\times\E=0$ on $\partial\Omega$ is problematic for three reasons.
First, the problem is an eigenproblem: the network must return an ordered set of modes whose eigenvalues, fields and normalisations are consistent,
and a plain regression loss gives no guarantee on any of them.
Second, the curl operator has an infinite-dimensional kernel formed by gradient fields;
any discretisation that does not respect the $H(\mathrm{curl})$ structure, and any predicted field that is not projected away from this kernel,
produces spurious modes at or near zero frequency \citep{hiptmair2002finite,boffi2010finite}.
Third, a network that outputs values on a fixed grid or a fixed set of degrees of freedom is tied to one discretisation,
whereas the accuracy of an eigenvalue computation depends on the mesh and the polynomial order.
Lowest-order edge elements add a limit: in our earlier lowest-order (Whitney) model, even the best representable field on the training mesh
had a \SIrange{12}{21}{\percent} error in $\E$ % src: docs/28 §1
and the frequency error floor was \num{2.4e-3}. % src: docs/28 §3

\paragraph{Approach.}
We therefore do not ask the network for the modes themselves.
The network maps the geometry to a \emph{learned basis} of $m$ vector fields.
A fixed linear map takes the basis to the degrees of freedom of a conforming high-order N\'ed\'elec space \citep{nedelec1980mixed} of order $p$ on a curved (isoparametric) mesh,
the basis is projected onto the discretely divergence-free subspace by solving a Poisson-type problem for the potential (the kernel projection, a float64 conjugate-gradient solve),
and a Rayleigh--Ritz step in the projected span returns the $K$ lowest frequencies and the fields.
The network is trained mainly to place the true modes inside the span of the basis; the Ritz step, not a regression head, then computes frequencies and fields.
Since this is a Galerkin projection onto a subspace of the discrete problem, min--max theory applies.
The construction is implemented with NGSolve and Netgen \citep{schoberl1997netgen,schoberl2014cpp11}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A neural operator with a built-in eigenvalue bound.}
  Every predicted eigenvalue is an upper bound of the corresponding nonzero discrete eigenvalue of the finite-element space in which the model runs,
  and the kernel projection excludes spurious zero modes (\cref{sec:method}, \cref{prop:upper-bound}).
  In the main held-out study every prediction stayed above the discrete eigenvalue of its space (smallest margin \SI{0.47}{\percent}); % src: docs/34 §8.2
  the bound is with respect to the discrete, not the exact, eigenvalue.
  \item \textbf{Independence of the discretisation space.}
  The same weights run unchanged on a $p=3$ space and on a mesh refined once (eight times as many tetrahedra), and the bound still holds
  (smallest margin \SI{0.009}{\percent}). % src: docs/34 §4.2
  Accuracy does not improve in the richer space (the frequency error worsens by \numrange{0.2}{0.6} percentage points): % src: docs/34 §4.2
  the remaining error lies in the learned basis.
  \item \textbf{Accuracy floor of the representation.}
  With quadrature-point tokens on a curved $p=2$ space, the relative eigenvalue error in a single-sample overfit test is \numrange{1e-6}{9e-6}, % src: docs/28 §3
  against \num{2.4e-3} for the lowest-order model. % src: docs/28 §3
  \item \textbf{A data pipeline with analytic validation.}
  An adaptive $p=3$ label generator on curved meshes with separate label and model meshes (\cref{sec:data}).
  On an analytic pillbox the solver reproduces the exact values to \num{6e-7} in frequency, \num{3e-5} in $\Qz$, \num{1e-5} in $\RoQ$, \num{2e-4} in $\Epk$ and \num{4e-4} in $\Bpk$ at \num{157000} degrees of freedom. % src: docs/31 §1
  Removing repeated work cut the cost of an adaptive label at \num{450000} DOF from \SI{664}{s} to \SI{433}{s}, with labels unchanged (frequency differences $\le\num{2e-15}$, figures of merit $\le\num{7e-9}$ relative). % src: docs/31 §3.8
  \item \textbf{Training on low-fidelity labels.}
  A low-fidelity (LF) label is one $p=2$ solve on the model mesh; the high-fidelity (HF) label is the adaptive $p=3$ solution projected to $p=2$ (\cref{sec:multifidelity}).
  An LF label is $4.3\times$ cheaper, % src: docs/34 §8.1
  differs from the HF label by \SIrange{0.004}{0.047}{\percent} in frequency and \SIrange{0.08}{0.32}{\percent} (mass norm) in the field subspace, % src: docs/34 §8.1
  and the same predictions score within $0.02$ percentage points against LF and against HF labels. % src: docs/34 §8.2
  In an equal-cost comparison on six held-out HF geometries, LF training at equal or lower label cost was at least as accurate as HF training, and more LF geometries helped slightly (\cref{tab:equal-cost}).
  These are single training runs with small differences; the LF--HF agreement is the robust result.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope.}
This is a method-and-pipeline paper with controlled small-scale evidence.
All training runs are small: CPU only, at most 40 training geometries from one family (a pillbox with beam pipes), and a 64-dimensional two-block trunk. % src: BRIEF §1; docs/34 §4, §8.2
The absolute errors of \SIrange{1}{2}{\percent} in frequency reflect this under-trained model rather than a limit of the method;
the figure-of-merit loss is switched off for the field model;
and fine-tuning on HF labels and mixed LF+HF training gave inconclusive results (\cref{sec:experiments}).
At the scale studied, inference takes a time of the same order as an FE solve in the same space (about \SI{8}{s} against \SI{18}{s}), % src: docs/34 §8.2 (5 s + 3.3 s vs 18 s)
and the model cannot be more accurate than the finite-element solution of its own space (\cref{sec:discussion}).
