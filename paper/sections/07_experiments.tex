\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

All experiments are small by design: CPU training, one geometry family, a small trunk. They isolate one design choice at a time
and do not probe the accuracy ceiling of the method. Frequency errors of one to two percent therefore reflect an
under-trained model, and only comparisons between arms that share model, family and evaluation protocol are evidence.
Every training arm is a single run (one seed); no confidence intervals are available.

\paragraph{Setup.}
% src: docs/34 §3, §4, §7, §8.2; configs/field_3d.yaml (random_seed 42, loss weights, kp_tol, drop_tol); src/training/lightning_module.py (custom_cosine, AdamW)
Everything ran on a shared four-core cloud VM without a GPU, often next to other jobs, so wall-clock times scatter. The model is that of \cref{sec:method} with embedding width 64, 2 attention blocks, 4 heads, basis width $\nbasis=12$ and $\nmodes=4$ predicted modes (labels store more). Training uses AdamW (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, gradient clipping 1), batch size 1, a learning rate that is constant for 10 epochs and then follows a cosine to $10^{-6}$, 600 steps per arm, one thread per run, seed 42, one run per arm; evaluation uses the last checkpoint. All settings are in \cref{tab:A07-hyper}.


\emph{Data.} All geometries are of the family \texttt{pillbox\_pipes} (pillbox cavities with pipes), drawn from a fixed-seed Sobol sequence, so identifier~$i$ is the same
geometry in the LF and HF sets (checked on four samples). The minimum-fillet parameter is $0.12$, whereas the generator default for field labels is $0.05$; this rounds the geometry and limits what the results say about sharper cavities.
% src: EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 10 (default 0.05); docs/34 §3 (0.05 -> 0.12)
Training sets: identifiers 0--7 (HF, 8 geometries), 0--23 (LF, 24), and 0--23 together with 30--45 (LF, 40). The six test geometries are
identifiers 24--29 with HF labels; their model meshes have 4.8k--10.0k tetrahedra. \emph{HF means one third-order solve on the start mesh}: the $150\,000$-DOF budget is used up by the first solve, so label mesh and model mesh coincide and the adaptive loop and the label/model-mesh split of \cref{sec:data} are not exercised by any training experiment.
% src: docs/34 §3 (adapt_iters = 1, adapt_stop = budget), §8.1
LF meshes equal HF model meshes byte for byte for the 8 HF training and the 6 test geometries (\cref{sec:multifidelity}); the 16 extra geometries of LF~40 have no HF counterpart and were generated with the same start-mesh rule.
% src: docs/34 §8.1 (8/8 and 6/6; 40 LF samples mean 7639 tets)

\emph{Metrics.} The frequency error of mode~$k$ is $f_k^{\mathrm{model}}/f_k^{\HF}-1$ against the stored HF label; where the evaluated space differs
from the label space, and to test \cref{prop:upper-bound}, it is also formed against the finite-element eigenvalue of the evaluated space, $f_k^{\mathrm{model}}/f_k^{\mathrm{FE}}-1$.
The field error is the sign-minimised relative $L^2$ error in the mass norm against the finite-element mode of the evaluated space (a subspace distance inside a cluster of near-degenerate modes).
Frequency statistics include all four predicted modes (0--3); field statistics use modes 0--2 only, because mode~3 is not yet learned
(field error $0.07$--$1.3$ in the study of \cref{sec:A07-first}, cause not investigated), and its frequency errors are the largest of the four, so they inflate the frequency means.
% src: docs/34 §4.1 (mode 3), §8.2 (statistics definition); EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 11

\subsection{Single-sample representation capacity}\label{sec:07-floor}
% src: docs/28 §3
We fit one curved pillbox (179 tetrahedra, 2280 DOF at order 2) to its own labels with a width-64 two-block trunk, $\nbasis=12$, $\nmodes=4$. The relative eigenvalue error $\theta_k/\lambda_k^{\mathrm{label}}-1$ against the label of the same space saturates at $0.8$--$1.1\times10^{-4}$ after 2000 steps with vertex tokens and barycentric interpolation (order 2; $2.3$--$3.0\times10^{-4}$ at order 3, 5480 DOF), and reaches $1$--$9\times10^{-6}$ after 1250 steps with the quadrature-point tokens of \cref{sec:method}.
% src: docs/28 §3
This is a capacity test on one training sample, not a generalisation result. The $2.4\times10^{-3}$ reported for the lowest-order Whitney model on similar cavities is the error of that space against the exact solution on a different sample, so it is not comparable and we draw no conclusion from it.

\subsection{Kernel projection against a penalty}\label{sec:07-penalty}
% src: docs/31 §3.5
The kernel projection could be replaced by a penalty $\Kmat+s\,\Mmat\Gmat\,\mathsf{D}^{-1}\Gmat^{\top}\Mmat$ that raises the gradient directions out of the spectrum, without a CG solve. We tested this on a pillbox with 13\,052 tetrahedra ($s=1964$), for fields with a given gradient mass fraction.
% src: docs/31 §3.5 (largest relative error vs target eigenvalues: gradient fraction 0 / 1e-6 / 1e-4 / 1e-2; projection 5e-6, 6e-6, 5e-6, 5e-6; penalty 4e-6, 6.6e-4, 6.7e-2, 6.7)
At fractions $0$, $10^{-6}$, $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-2}$ the largest relative eigenvalue error of the penalty is $4\times10^{-6}$, $6.6\times10^{-4}$, $6.7\times10^{-2}$ and $6.7$, whereas the projection stays at $5\times10^{-6}$ to $6\times10^{-6}$.
The penalty never gives a lower value than the projection, so an upper bound is retained, but a network output has no reason to keep its gradient content below $10^{-8}$, and the penalty was rejected.

\subsection{Training on low-fidelity labels at equal label cost}\label{sec:07-equal-cost}
% src: docs/34 §8.1, §8.2; docs/35 §5 (4.0)
This is the main experiment. All arms share model, family, test set, optimiser and 600 optimisation steps; they differ in the labels, and therefore also in the \emph{number} of training geometries (8, 24, 40), so fidelity and data size are confounded: an LF-8 arm on identifiers 0--7 was not run.
Arm~A trains on the 8 HF geometries (each listed five times per epoch; 15 epochs of 40 items). Arm~B2 uses 24 LF geometries (25 epochs of 24), about $20\%$ cheaper in label time than A.
Arm~C2 uses 40 LF geometries (15 epochs of 40, the schedule of A) at $1.4\times$ the label cost of A. Label cost is the measured time for mesh generation plus labelling on the shared VM
(two workers, one thread each, next to a training job); the HF/LF cost ratio of the same eight meshes was $4.3$ under that load, and $2.8$--$3.7$ in a fresh container without training load; a later re-measurement on the six test geometries gave $4.0$ (three workers, one thread each) (\cref{sec:multifidelity}). Arm~D continues C2 for 200 steps on the 8 HF geometries.

\paragraph{Result.} The three arms are comparable (\cref{tab:equal-cost}). At $20\%$ lower label cost, B2 has a mean error of $1.55\%$ against $1.66\%$ for A, but the difference comes from the outlier geometry~27: without it the means are $1.11\%$ (A), $1.14\%$ (B2) and $1.06\%$ (C2).
C2, at $1.4\times$ the cost, is lower than A on three of the six geometries (24, 27, 29) and $0.01$--$0.06$ points behind on the other three (\cref{fig:07-pergeom}; values in \cref{tab:A07-pergeom}). The differences are small against the spread over geometries and each arm is one run, so we do not rank the arms.
Geometry~27 is the worst in every arm ($2.9$--$4.4\%$); we did not investigate why. The supported statement is therefore that LF labels at equal or lower cost gave a comparable held-out error in this setting, not that they are more accurate.
% src: docs/34 §8.2

\paragraph{What this experiment can and cannot show.} On identical meshes the LF and HF frequencies differ by at most $0.047\%$ and the fields by at most $0.32\%$ in the mass norm (modes 0--3), whereas the model errors are $1$--$2\%$ in frequency and about $5\%$ in field: the label difference is one to two orders of magnitude smaller than the model error (at least about $15\times$ for the field, $20\times$ for the frequency).
% src: docs/34 §8.1 (0.004-0.047 %, 0.08-0.32 %); §8.2 (model error); the factor is computed from mean errors 1.1-1.7 % vs 0.047 % (frequency 23-425x, field 14-63x: one to two orders of magnitude)
With the model this far from the labels, the study does not probe the regime in which the label type should matter, and the confound with the number of geometries remains. The robust results are on the label side (\cref{sec:multifidelity}): LF labels on the HF model mesh are cheaper by $2.8$--$4.3\times$ and nearly identical to the HF labels. A parametric-regression baseline (\cref{sec:07-baselines}) is equally insensitive to the label fidelity, which suggests that at this scale the number of training geometries decides the accuracy.

\paragraph{LF-to-HF gap.} The predictions of C2 have a mean error of $1.35\%$ against the LF labels of the test meshes and $1.37\%$ against the HF labels: $0.02$ percentage points apart. This is a consequence of the label agreement above, not independent evidence that training on LF labels is harmless.
% src: docs/34 §8.2

\paragraph{Ritz bound.} In every evaluation of this experiment (all arms, geometries and modes) the predicted eigenvalues lie above the finite-element eigenvalue of the evaluated space; the smallest margin $f_k^{\mathrm{model}}/f_k^{\mathrm{FE}}-1$ is $+0.47\%$ (\cref{prop:upper-bound}). This is a consistency check only, since model errors are far larger than the margin.
Because the implemented projection is inexact, the guarantee was also probed on two test meshes (4818 and 6315 tetrahedra) with synthetic bases and several CG tolerances $\tau$. At the shipped $\tau=10^{-8}$ the bound $\theta_k\ge\lambda_{h,k}$ was never violated for exact eigenvectors with noise or for random bases; it was violated only for bases with a gradient admixture, by at most $1.5\times10^{-8}$ (relative) at gradient mass fraction $0.99$ and $1.4\times10^{-10}$ at $0.5$ (a trained basis has about $0.5$--$0.6$ before projection), always within the a posteriori perturbation $\bar\eta\approx(1\text{--}3)\,\tau$. At $\tau=10^{-4}$ violations reach $8\times10^{-5}$ and the drop rule fails for nearly pure gradient directions; it protects only for $\tau\lesssim10^{-6}$ (\cref{sec:method}).
% src: docs/35 §2 (stress test, geometries 24 and 27); docs/34 §8.2 (+0.47 %)
Assumption~(A) of \cref{sec:method} was verified on five small curved meshes with $p=1,2,3$ (15 cases, including a two-wall shell); production meshes were checked only indirectly.
% src: docs/35 §1

\paragraph{Fine-tuning and mixed training are inconclusive.} Arm~D improves on C2 (mean error $1.37\%\to1.13\%$), but the gain cannot be attributed to the HF labels: the control (same start, same 200 steps, LF data only) was lost when the container restarted before its test evaluation; on the three validation geometries it had a mean absolute frequency error of $0.031$\,GHz and a field error of $0.046$, against $0.029$\,GHz and $0.052$ for D, a mixed signal. Two further runs are excluded: a mixed LF-40 plus HF-8 run ($8\times80$ steps) never entered its decay phase (eight epochs is fewer than the ten-epoch constant phase) and reached $1.95\%$, and a mixed LF-32 plus HF-8 run was lost on restart at epoch~6.
% src: docs/34 §8.2 (invalid and lost arms)
We make no claim about fine-tuning or mixed training. In an earlier, smaller study (\cref{sec:A07-first}), a fine-tune with a proper control gave no gain from HF labels: the improvement ($1.8\%\to1.5\%$) came from the extra epochs.
% src: docs/34 §4.3

\subsection{Transfer to other spaces}\label{sec:07-transfer}
% src: docs/34 §4.2
The Ritz layer uses only $\Kmat,\Mmat,\Gmat,\Kp$ of the space it is given, so trained weights can be evaluated in another conforming space without retraining. \Cref{tab:A07-transfer} (appendix) does this for the model of the earlier study (\cref{sec:A07-first}) on two HF geometries: the native order-2 space, order~3 on the same mesh and, for geometry~5, order~2 on the once uniformly refined mesh (two threads). The frequency errors against the HF labels are $+0.63$ to $+2.51\%$ natively, $+0.88$ to $+3.23\%$ at order~3 and $+0.86$ to $+2.29\%$ on the refined mesh. In all spaces the predicted eigenvalues lay above the finite-element eigenvalue of the evaluated space. Accuracy does not improve: the frequency error is $0.2$--$0.7$ percentage points \emph{worse} than natively and the field error is unchanged.
% src: docs/34 §4.2 table (differences computed from the table: id 3 p3 +0.68/+0.34/+0.35/+0.72; id 5 p3 +0.59/+0.25/+0.26/+0.64; refined +0.60/+0.23/+0.21/+0.40; EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 6; docs/34's "0.2-0.6" was a misstatement)
We attribute this to the learned basis $\psi_j$ being the same smooth field in a richer space and to the different token density (24 quadrature points per tetrahedron at order~3 against 14 at order~2); neither was tested. Transfer thus establishes validity (the guarantee and the absence of spurious modes survive a change of space), not accuracy.

\subsection{Baselines}\label{sec:07-baselines}
% src: docs/35 §3, §4; docs/34 §8.2
Two baselines test whether the model is needed on this family. Both were evaluated on the six HF test geometries with the statistics of \cref{tab:equal-cost} (24 values); the model numbers are the single runs of \cref{tab:equal-cost}, not re-run.

It returns frequencies only, no fields.

\paragraph{Reading.} On this family and at these sizes plain finite elements dominate the model in cost and accuracy: order 1 on the model mesh needs $1.7$\,s for a mean error of $1.25\%$, against $8.3$\,s for $1.4$--$1.7\%$ for the model, and order 2 on the coarser mesh needs $5.7$\,s for $0.037\%$. The order-2 solve on the model mesh took $13.9$\,s on average ($7.4$\,s on geometry 24); the model time comes from another run and geometry, so we make no speed claim for the model.
Together, the baselines show that on one low-dimensional family the model has no accuracy advantage over parametric regression once 24 or more geometries are available, and no cost or accuracy advantage over direct finite elements. They do not test the settings in which the approach could pay off, which remain hypotheses: geometry sets without a shared low-dimensional parametrisation (arbitrary CAD), predicted fields and figures of merit together with a guaranteed eigenvalue ordering, warm starts for large finite-element solves, and larger meshes on which solver cost grows faster than the model's (\cref{sec:discussion}). A GPU timing of the model was not measured.

\subsection{Training-path engineering}\label{sec:07-engineering}
% src: docs/31 §3.6, §3.7; docs/28 §4
Removing repeated, parameter-independent work cut the single-thread training step of the 8931-tetrahedron pillbox from $40.7$ to $32.4$\,s ($-20\%$), and activation checkpointing reduced the peak memory of a 13k-tetrahedron step from $9.9$ to $3.8$\,GB, with unchanged results (\cref{sec:A07-engineering}).
