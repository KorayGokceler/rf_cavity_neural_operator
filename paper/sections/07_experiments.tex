\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

All experiments are small by design: CPU training, one geometry family, a small trunk. They isolate one design choice at a time
and do not probe the accuracy ceiling of the method. Frequency errors of one to two percent therefore reflect an
under-trained model, and only comparisons between arms that share model, family and evaluation protocol are evidence.
Every training arm is a single run (one seed); no confidence intervals are available.

\emph{Data.} All geometries are of the family \texttt{pillbox\_pipes} (pillbox cavities with pipes), drawn from a fixed-seed Sobol sequence, so identifier~$i$ is the same
geometry in the LF and HF sets (checked on four samples). The minimum-fillet parameter is $0.12$, whereas the generator default for field labels is $0.05$; this rounds the geometry and limits what the results say about sharper cavities.
% src: EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 10 (default 0.05); docs/34 §3 (0.05 -> 0.12)
Training sets: identifiers 0--7 (HF, 8 geometries), 0--23 (LF, 24), and 0--23 together with 30--45 (LF, 40). The six test geometries are
identifiers 24--29 with HF labels; their model meshes have 4.8k--10.0k tetrahedra. \emph{HF means one third-order solve on the start mesh}: the $150\,000$-DOF budget is used up by the first solve, so label mesh and model mesh coincide and the adaptive loop and the label/model-mesh split of \cref{sec:data} are not exercised by any training experiment.
% src: docs/34 §3 (adapt_iters = 1, adapt_stop = budget), §8.1
LF meshes equal HF model meshes byte for byte for the 8 HF training and the 6 test geometries (\cref{sec:multifidelity}); the 16 extra geometries of LF~40 have no HF counterpart and were generated with the same start-mesh rule.
% src: docs/34 §8.1 (8/8 and 6/6; 40 LF samples mean 7639 tets)

\emph{Metrics.} The frequency error of mode~$k$ is $f_k^{\mathrm{model}}/f_k^{\HF}-1$ against the stored HF label; where the evaluated space differs
from the label space, and to test \cref{prop:upper-bound}, it is also formed against the finite-element eigenvalue of the evaluated space, $f_k^{\mathrm{model}}/f_k^{\mathrm{FE}}-1$.
The field error is the relative $L^2$ error in the mass norm against the finite-element mode of the evaluated space, minimised over the sign for an isolated mode,
and the relative distance between target and predicted subspace inside a cluster of near-degenerate modes.
Frequency statistics include all four predicted modes (0--3); field statistics use modes 0--2 only, because mode~3 is not yet learned
(field error $0.07$--$1.3$ in the study of \cref{sec:A07-first}, cause not investigated), and its frequency errors are the largest of the four, so they inflate the frequency means.
% src: docs/34 §4.1 (mode 3), §8.2 (statistics definition); EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 11

\subsection{Single-sample representation capacity}\label{sec:07-floor}
% src: docs/28 §3
\Cref{tab:07-overfit} fits one curved pillbox (179 tetrahedra) to its own order-2 labels with a width-64 two-block trunk, $\nbasis=12$, $\nmodes=4$. With vertex tokens and barycentric interpolation
the eigenvalue error saturates near $10^{-4}$ (worse at order~3); with the quadrature-point tokens of \cref{sec:method} it reaches $10^{-6}$--$9\times10^{-6}$ in fewer steps.
This is a capacity test on one training sample, not a generalisation result. The lowest-order Whitney (N0) model was reported at a frequency error of $2.4\times10^{-3}$ on similar cavities, but that is the error of the space against the exact solution on a different sample, so it is not a like-for-like comparison and we draw no conclusion from it.

\subsection{Kernel projection against a penalty}\label{sec:07-penalty}
% src: docs/31 §3.5
The kernel projection could be replaced by a penalty $\Kmat+s\,\Mmat\Gmat\,\mathsf{D}^{-1}\Gmat^{\top}\Mmat$ that raises the gradient directions out of the spectrum, without a CG solve. We tested this on a pillbox with 13\,052 tetrahedra ($s=1964$, chosen from the extreme eigenvalues of the potential problem), for fields with a given gradient mass fraction (\cref{tab:07-penalty}).
The penalty stays above the target eigenvalues, so the bound is kept, but its error grows with the gradient content of the field, from $4\times10^{-6}$ to $6.7$ at a fraction of $10^{-2}$, whereas the projection stays at $5\times10^{-6}$. A network output has no reason to keep its gradient content below $10^{-8}$, so the penalty was rejected.

\subsection{Training on low-fidelity labels at equal label cost}\label{sec:07-equal-cost}
% src: docs/34 §8.1, §8.2
This is the main experiment. All arms share model, family, test set, optimiser and 600 optimisation steps; they differ in the labels, and therefore also in the \emph{number} of training geometries (8, 24, 40), so fidelity and data size are confounded: an LF-8 arm on identifiers 0--7 was not run.
Arm~A trains on the 8 HF geometries (each listed five times per epoch; 15 epochs of 40 items). Arm~B2 uses 24 LF geometries (25 epochs of 24), about $20\%$ cheaper in label time than A.
Arm~C2 uses 40 LF geometries (15 epochs of 40, the schedule of A) at $1.4\times$ the label cost of A. Label cost is the measured time for mesh generation plus labelling on the shared VM
(two workers, one thread each, next to a training job); the HF/LF cost ratio of the same eight meshes was $4.3$ under that load, and $2.8$--$3.7$ in a fresh container without training load (\cref{sec:multifidelity}). Arm~D continues C2 for 200 steps on the 8 HF geometries.

\paragraph{Result.} The three arms are comparable (\cref{tab:equal-cost}). At $20\%$ lower label cost, B2 has a mean error of $1.55\%$ against $1.66\%$ for A, but the difference comes from the outlier geometry~27: without it the means are $1.11\%$ (A), $1.14\%$ (B2) and $1.06\%$ (C2).
C2, at $1.4\times$ the cost, is lower than A on three of the six geometries (24, 27, 29) and $0.01$--$0.06$ points behind on the other three (\cref{tab:07-pergeom}). The differences are small against the spread over geometries and each arm is one run, so we do not rank the arms.
Geometry~27 is the worst in every arm ($2.9$--$4.4\%$); we did not investigate why. The supported statement is therefore that LF labels at equal or lower cost gave a comparable held-out error in this setting, not that they are more accurate.
% src: docs/34 §8.2

\paragraph{What this experiment can and cannot show.} On identical meshes the LF and HF frequencies differ by at most $0.047\%$ and the fields by at most $0.32\%$ in the mass norm (modes 0--3), whereas the model errors are $1$--$2\%$ in frequency and about $5\%$ in field: the label difference is more than twenty times smaller than the model error.
% src: docs/34 §8.1 (0.004-0.047 %, 0.08-0.32 %); §8.2 (model error); the factor is computed from mean errors 1.1-1.7 % vs 0.047 % (EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 2 quotes 30-300x)
With the model this far from the labels, the study does not probe the regime in which the label type should matter, and the confound with the number of geometries remains. The robust results are on the label side (\cref{sec:multifidelity}): LF labels on the HF model mesh are cheaper by $2.8$--$4.3\times$ and nearly identical to the HF labels.

\paragraph{LF-to-HF gap.} The predictions of C2 have a mean error of $1.35\%$ against the LF labels of the test meshes and $1.37\%$ against the HF labels: $0.02$ percentage points apart. This is a consequence of the label agreement above, not independent evidence that training on LF labels is harmless.
% src: docs/34 §8.2

\paragraph{Ritz bound.} In every evaluation of this experiment (all arms, geometries and modes) the predicted eigenvalues lie above the finite-element eigenvalue of the evaluated space; the smallest margin $f_k^{\mathrm{model}}/f_k^{\mathrm{FE}}-1$ is $+0.47\%$ (\cref{prop:upper-bound}). The model errors are so much larger than the margin required by the bound that this is a consistency check and not a stress test of the guarantee.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning and mixed training are inconclusive.} Arm~D improves on C2 (mean error $1.37\%\to1.13\%$), but the gain cannot be attributed to the HF labels. The control (same start, same 200 steps, LF data only) was lost when the container restarted before its test evaluation;
on the three validation geometries it had a mean absolute frequency error of $0.031$\,GHz and a field error of $0.046$, against $0.029$\,GHz and $0.052$ for D, a mixed signal. Two further runs are excluded: a mixed LF-40 plus HF-8 run with $8\times80$ steps never entered its decay phase
(eight epochs is fewer than the ten-epoch constant phase) and reached $1.95\%$, and a mixed LF-32 plus HF-8 run with the same schedule was lost on restart at epoch~6.
% src: docs/34 §8.2 (invalid and lost arms)
We make no claim about fine-tuning or mixed training. An earlier, smaller study (14 LF training geometries, evaluation on four HF geometries; \cref{sec:A07-first}) found frequency errors of $0.63$--$3.02\%$ on HF geometries, all positive, an order-2 floor within $0.033\%$ of the HF labels (so the error is the model's), and no gain from a fine-tune with a proper control: the improvement ($1.8\%\to1.5\%$) came from the extra epochs.
% src: docs/34 §4.1, §4.3

In all spaces the predicted eigenvalues lay above the finite-element eigenvalue of the evaluated space. Accuracy does not improve: the frequency error is $0.2$--$0.7$ percentage points \emph{worse} than natively and the field error is unchanged.

We attribute this to the learned basis $\psi_j$ being the same smooth field in a richer space, so that the error comes from the basis and not from the space, and to the different token density (24 quadrature points per tetrahedron at order~3 against 14 at order~2), a small distribution shift; neither explanation was tested.
Transfer thus establishes validity (the guarantee and the absence of spurious modes survive a change of space), not accuracy.

\subsection{Inference cost}\label{sec:07-cost}
% src: docs/34 §4.2, §8.2; EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 7
In the equal-cost study (one thread) the operators took $5.0$\,s and the forward pass $3.3$\,s ($8.3$\,s in total), against $18$\,s for assembling and solving the finite-element problem in the same space: at these sizes the model is about twice as fast, not more.
The larger ratios of \cref{tab:07-transfer} (two threads: $2.2$--$2.7$ at order~3 and $3.5$ on the refined mesh, $54$\,s against $192$\,s; ratios computed from the table) come with a model error of $1$--$2\%$, whereas the solve is exact up to the discretisation error of its space. We claim no speed advantage beyond this.
We have not compared with a cheap coarse-mesh finite-element solve at a similar accuracy, nor with a regression on the few geometry parameters of this family; either may well dominate the model at this scale.

\subsection{Training-path engineering}\label{sec:07-engineering}
% src: docs/31 §3.6, §3.7; docs/28 §4
Removing repeated, parameter-independent work (stored wall features, a transpose prepared once per geometry, preallocated buffers in the projection solve) cut the single-thread training step of the 8931-tetrahedron pillbox from $40.7$ to $32.4$\,s ($-20\%$); activation checkpointing reduced the peak memory of a 13k-tetrahedron step from $9.9$ to $3.8$\,GB at an unchanged step time on CPU.
The results are unchanged (details, a smaller gain on a smaller cell and the options not adopted: \cref{sec:A07-engineering}); no GPU timing was made.
