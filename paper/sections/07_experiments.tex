\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

All experiments are small by design: CPU training, one geometry family, a small trunk. Frequency errors of one to two percent reflect an under-trained model, not the ceiling of the method, and only comparisons between arms that share model, family and protocol are evidence. Every training arm is a single run; no confidence intervals are available.

\paragraph{Setup.}
% src: docs/34 §3, §4, §7, §8.2; configs/field_3d.yaml (random_seed 42, loss weights, kp_tol, drop_tol); src/training/lightning_module.py (custom_cosine, AdamW)
Everything ran on a shared four-core cloud VM without a GPU, often next to other jobs, so wall-clock times scatter. The model is that of \cref{sec:method} with embedding width 64, 2 attention blocks, 4 heads, basis width $\nbasis=12$ and $\nmodes=4$ predicted modes (labels store more). Training uses AdamW (learning rate $10^{-3}$, constant for 10 epochs, then cosine), batch size 1, 600 steps per arm, one thread, seed 42, one run per arm; evaluation uses the last checkpoint (all settings: \cref{tab:A07-hyper}). Reductions of the training-path cost (step $-20\%$, peak memory $9.9\to3.8$\,GB, results unchanged) are in \cref{sec:A07-engineering}.
% src: docs/31 §3.6, §3.7


\emph{Data.} All geometries are of the family \texttt{pillbox\_pipes} (pillbox cavities with pipes), drawn from a fixed-seed Sobol sequence, so identifier~$i$ is the same
geometry in the LF and HF sets (checked on four samples). The minimum-fillet parameter is $0.12$ (generator default for field labels: $0.05$), which rounds the geometry.
% src: EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 10 (default 0.05); docs/34 §3 (0.05 -> 0.12)
Training sets: identifiers 0--7 (HF, 8 geometries), 0--23 (LF, 24), 0--23 and 30--45 (LF, 40); test: identifiers 24--29 with HF labels (4.8k--10.0k tetrahedra). \emph{HF means one third-order solve on the start mesh}: the $150\,000$-DOF budget is used up by the first solve, so label mesh and model mesh coincide and the adaptive loop and the label/model-mesh split of \cref{sec:data} are not exercised by any training experiment.
% src: docs/34 §3 (adapt_iters = 1, adapt_stop = budget), §8.1
LF and HF model meshes coincide for the 8 HF training and the 6 test geometries (\cref{sec:multifidelity}).
% src: docs/34 §8.1 (8/8 and 6/6; 40 LF samples mean 7639 tets)

\emph{Metrics.} The frequency error of mode~$k$ is $f_k^{\mathrm{model}}/f_k^{\HF}-1$ against the stored HF label; to test \cref{prop:upper-bound} it is also formed against the finite-element eigenvalue of the evaluated space, $f_k^{\mathrm{model}}/f_k^{\mathrm{FE}}-1$.
The field error is the sign-minimised relative $L^2$ error in the mass norm against the finite-element mode of the evaluated space (a subspace distance inside a cluster of near-degenerate modes).
Frequency statistics include all four predicted modes (0--3); field statistics use modes 0--2 only, because mode~3 is not yet learned (field error $0.07$--$1.3$ in \cref{sec:A07-first}) and has the largest frequency errors, which inflate the frequency means.
% src: docs/34 §4.1 (mode 3), §8.2 (statistics definition); EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 11

\subsection{Single-sample representation capacity and the projection}\label{sec:07-floor}\label{sec:07-penalty}
% src: docs/28 §3
We fit one curved pillbox (179 tetrahedra, 2280 DOF at order 2) to its own labels with a width-64 two-block trunk, $\nbasis=12$, $\nmodes=4$. The relative eigenvalue error $\theta_k/\lambda_k^{\mathrm{label}}-1$ against the label of the same space saturates at $0.8$--$1.1\times10^{-4}$ after 2000 steps with vertex tokens and barycentric interpolation (order 2; $2.3$--$3.0\times10^{-4}$ at order 3, 5480 DOF), and reaches $1$--$9\times10^{-6}$ after 1250 steps with the quadrature-point tokens of \cref{sec:method}.
% src: docs/28 §3
This is a capacity test on one training sample, not a generalisation result; the $2.4\times10^{-3}$ reported for the lowest-order Whitney model is the error of that space against the exact solution on a different sample and is not comparable. A penalty replacing the projection lost accuracy as soon as the field contained gradients (error $6.7\times10^{-2}$ at gradient mass fraction $10^{-4}$, against $5\times10^{-6}$ for the projection; \cref{sec:A07-penalty}).
% src: docs/31 §3.5

\subsection{Training on low-fidelity labels at equal label cost}\label{sec:07-equal-cost}
% src: docs/34 §8.1, §8.2; docs/35 §5 (4.0)
This is the main experiment. All arms share model, family, test set, optimiser and 600 optimisation steps; they differ in the labels, and therefore also in the \emph{number} of training geometries (8, 24, 40), so fidelity and data size are confounded: an LF-8 arm on identifiers 0--7 was not run.
Arm~A trains on the 8 HF geometries (each listed five times per epoch; 15 epochs of 40 items). Arm~B2 uses 24 LF geometries (25 epochs of 24), about $20\%$ cheaper in label time than A.
Arm~C2 uses 40 LF geometries (15 epochs of 40, the schedule of A) at $1.4\times$ the label cost of A. Label cost is the measured time for mesh generation plus labelling on the shared VM (two workers, one thread each, next to a training job); the HF/LF cost ratio of the same eight meshes was $4.3$ under that load, $2.8$--$3.7$ in a fresh container without training load, and $4.0$ in a later re-measurement on the six test geometries (\cref{sec:multifidelity}). Arm~D continues C2 for 200 steps on the 8 HF geometries.

\paragraph{Result.} The three arms are comparable (\cref{tab:equal-cost}). At $20\%$ lower label cost, B2 has a mean error of $1.55\%$ against $1.66\%$ for A, but the difference comes from the outlier geometry~27: without it the means are $1.11\%$ (A), $1.14\%$ (B2) and $1.06\%$ (C2).
C2, at $1.4\times$ the cost, is lower than A on three of the six geometries (24, 27, 29) and $0.01$--$0.06$ points behind on the other three (\cref{fig:07-pergeom}; values in \cref{tab:A07-pergeom}). The differences are small against the spread over geometries and each arm is one run, so we do not rank the arms; geometry~27 is the worst in every arm ($2.9$--$4.4\%$, cause not investigated). LF labels at equal or lower cost gave a comparable held-out error here, not a better one.
% src: docs/34 §8.2

\paragraph{What this experiment can and cannot show.} On identical meshes the LF and HF frequencies differ by at most $0.047\%$ and the fields by at most $0.32\%$ in the mass norm (modes 0--3), whereas model errors are $1$--$2\%$ in frequency and about $5\%$ in field: the label difference is at least $15\times$ (field) to $20\times$ (frequency) smaller than the model error.
% src: docs/34 §8.1 (0.004-0.047 %, 0.08-0.32 %); §8.2 (model error); factors computed from mean errors 1.1-1.7 % vs 0.047 % (frequency 23-425x, field 14-63x)
The study therefore does not probe the regime in which the label type should matter, and the confound with the number of geometries remains. The mean error of C2 is $1.35\%$ against the LF labels of the test meshes and $1.37\%$ against the HF labels; this $0.02$-point gap follows from the label agreement and is not independent evidence that LF training is harmless.
% src: docs/34 §8.2
The robust results are on the label side (\cref{sec:multifidelity}): LF labels on the HF model mesh are $2.8$--$4.3\times$ cheaper and nearly identical to the HF labels. A parametric-regression baseline (\cref{sec:07-baselines}) is equally insensitive to label fidelity, so at this scale the number of training geometries decides the accuracy.

\paragraph{Ritz bound.} In every evaluation of this experiment (all arms, geometries, modes) the predicted eigenvalues lie above the finite-element eigenvalue of the evaluated space; the smallest margin $f_k^{\mathrm{model}}/f_k^{\mathrm{FE}}-1$ is $+0.47\%$ (\cref{prop:upper-bound}). This is only a consistency check, since model errors far exceed the margin. As the implemented projection is inexact, the guarantee was also probed with synthetic bases and several CG tolerances (\cref{sec:A07-ritz}): at the shipped tolerance $10^{-8}$ the bound was violated only for bases with a gradient admixture, by at most $1.5\times10^{-8}$ (relative), within the a posteriori perturbation $\bar\eta\approx(1\text{--}3)\,\tau$; assumption~(A) of \cref{sec:method} held in 15 small cases.
% src: docs/35 §1, §2; docs/34 §8.2 (+0.47 %)

\paragraph{Fine-tuning and mixed training are inconclusive.} Arm~D improves on C2 ($1.37\%\to1.13\%$), but its control (same start and 200 steps, LF data only) was lost on a container restart before its test evaluation, and on three validation geometries the two were mixed (frequency error $0.031$\,GHz for the control against $0.029$ for D; field error $0.046$ against $0.052$), so the gain cannot be attributed to the HF labels. Two mixed LF+HF runs are excluded (one never left the constant-learning-rate phase, $1.95\%$; one was lost on restart). We make no claim about fine-tuning or mixed training; in an earlier study (\cref{sec:A07-first}) a fine-tune with a proper control gave no gain from HF labels.
% src: docs/34 §8.2 (invalid and lost arms), §4.3

\subsection{Transfer to other spaces}\label{sec:07-transfer}
% src: docs/34 §4.2 table (differences computed from the table: id 3 p3 +0.68/+0.34/+0.35/+0.72; id 5 p3 +0.59/+0.25/+0.26/+0.64; refined +0.60/+0.23/+0.21/+0.40; EDITOR_NOTES_2 decision 6; docs/34's "0.2-0.6" was a misstatement)
Since the Ritz layer uses only $\Kmat,\Mmat,\Gmat,\Kp$ of the space it is given, trained weights can be evaluated in another conforming space. For the model of the earlier study (\cref{sec:A07-first}) on two HF geometries, order~3 on the same mesh and order~2 on the once uniformly refined mesh kept the predicted eigenvalues above the finite-element eigenvalue of the evaluated space, but accuracy did not improve: the frequency error was $0.2$--$0.7$ percentage points \emph{worse} than natively and the field error unchanged (\cref{tab:A07-transfer}). We attribute this to the learned basis being the same smooth field in a richer space and to the different token density (24 against 14 points per tetrahedron), both untested; transfer establishes validity, not accuracy.

With 24 or more geometries the GP matches or beats the model: with LF 40, mean and maximum $1.01\%$ and $1.82\%$ against $1.37\%$ and $5.30\%$ (only the median is higher), with LF 24 $1.64\%$ against $1.55\%$; on geometry 27 the GP has $0.92\%$ where the model has $2.9$--$4.4\%$. Caveats: one family, no model seeds; geometries 24 and 27 lie slightly outside the training parameter box.

\paragraph{Reading.} At these sizes plain finite elements dominate the model in cost and accuracy: order 1 on the model mesh needs $1.7$\,s for a mean error of $1.25\%$, against $8.3$\,s for $1.4$--$1.7\%$ for the model, and order 2 on the coarser mesh $5.7$\,s for $0.037\%$. The model time comes from another run and geometry, so we make no speed claim for the model.
So on one low-dimensional family the model has no accuracy advantage over parametric regression once 24 or more geometries are available, and no cost or accuracy advantage over direct finite elements. The baselines do not test the settings in which the approach could pay off, which remain hypotheses: geometry sets without a shared low-dimensional parametrisation, predicted fields and figures of merit with a guaranteed eigenvalue ordering, warm starts for large finite-element solves, and larger meshes (\cref{sec:discussion}). No GPU timing was made.
