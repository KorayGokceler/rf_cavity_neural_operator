\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

All experiments are small by design. They run on CPU, on one geometry family, with a small trunk, and they
are meant to isolate the effect of one design choice at a time, not to reach the accuracy ceiling of the
method. Absolute errors of one to two percent in frequency therefore reflect an under-trained model, and only
comparisons between arms that share the model, the data family and the evaluation protocol should be read as evidence.

\paragraph{Setup.}
% src: docs/34 §3, §4, §7, §8.2; docs/31 §3.9; configs/field_3d.yaml
Training and evaluation ran on a shared four-core cloud VM without a GPU, usually with other jobs running at
the same time, so wall-clock times carry a noticeable spread. The equal-cost experiment used one compute thread
per run, the earlier studies two. The model is the field model of \cref{sec:method} with embedding width~64,
two attention blocks, four heads, a learned basis of width $\nbasis=12$, 32 random Fourier features and
$\nmodes=4$ predicted modes. It is trained with AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled} (implemented in
PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}), learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, gradient-norm clipping at~1 and
batch size~1. The learning rate is constant for the first ten epochs and follows a cosine decay to $10^{-6}$ over the remaining
epochs of the run (an epoch is one pass over the training list, so the decay is only active if a run has
more than ten epochs). The loss is the span loss in the mass-plus-energy norm, with the self-supervised, orthogonality and frequency terms
of \cref{sec:method} at their default weights; the figure-of-merit loss is switched off for the field model.
Dropout is zero and no data augmentation is used.

\emph{Data.} All geometries belong to the family \texttt{pillbox\_pipes} (pillbox cavities with pipes) with minimum-fillet
parameter of the generator set to $0.12$, drawn from a Sobol sequence with a fixed seed, so that identifier~$i$ is the same
geometry in the low- and the high-fidelity set (checked on four samples). Training uses identifiers 0--7 (HF, 8 geometries),
0--23 (LF, 24) and 0--23 plus 30--45 (LF, 40); the six test geometries are identifiers 24--29 and
carry HF labels. In the equal-cost experiment the stored model mesh of every test geometry has between
4.8k and 10.0k tetrahedra, and the LF label meshes of the training geometries coincide with the HF model meshes
(\cref{sec:multifidelity}).

\emph{Metrics.} The frequency error of mode~$k$ is $f_k^{\mathrm{model}}/f_k^{\HF}-1$, with $f^{\HF}_k$ the stored
label. Where the evaluated space differs from the label space, or to test the bound of \cref{prop:upper-bound}, it is also
computed against the finite-element eigenvalue of the evaluated space itself, $f^{\mathrm{model}}_k/f^{\mathrm{FE}}_k-1$. The
field error is the relative $L^2$ error in the mass norm of the predicted mode against the finite-element mode of the evaluated space,
minimised over the sign for an isolated mode, and the relative distance between the target and the predicted subspace
inside a cluster of near-degenerate modes. We report it averaged over modes 0--2; the fourth mode is
not learned by this model (field error between 0.07 and 1.3 on the first study's geometries; cause not investigated) and is excluded.
Unless stated otherwise, all models are evaluated from the last checkpoint, without checkpoint selection, on the stored mesh in the order-2 space.

\subsection{Accuracy floor}\label{sec:07-floor}

% src: docs/28 §3
We first ask how accurately the architecture can represent the modes at all. \Cref{tab:07-overfit} overfits a single curved
pillbox (179 tetrahedra, span loss, width-64 two-block trunk, $\nbasis=12$, $\nmodes=4$). With vertex tokens and barycentric
interpolation of the learned fields, the relative eigenvalue error saturates around $10^{-4}$, and is worse at order~3. With the quadrature-point
tokens of \cref{sec:method} it reaches $10^{-6}$--$9\times10^{-6}$ in fewer steps. For comparison, the lowest-order
Whitney (N0) model has a relative frequency floor of $2.4\times10^{-3}$ on the same kind of cavities (not the same sample).
A single-sample fit measures representational capacity, not generalisation.

\subsection{Training on low-fidelity labels at equal label cost}\label{sec:07-equal-cost}

% src: docs/34 §8.2
This is the main experiment. All arms share the model, the family, the test set, the optimiser and the number of
optimisation steps (600); they differ in the labels. Arm~A trains on 8 HF geometries (listed five times per epoch,
$15\times40$ steps). Arm~B2 trains on 24 LF geometries ($25\times24$ steps), which costs about $20\%$ less
label time than A. Arm~C2 trains on 40 LF geometries ($15\times40$ steps, the same schedule as A) at $1.4\times$ the
label cost of A. Label cost is the measured wall-clock time of mesh generation plus labelling on the shared VM (two workers, one thread each, running next to a training job); the HF to LF cost ratio of the
same eight meshes is $4.3$ (\cref{sec:multifidelity}). Arm~D continues C2 for 200 steps on the 8 HF geometries at learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$.

\paragraph{Result.} Training on LF labels is not worse than training on HF labels at equal or lower label cost, and is
slightly better in the arm that spends somewhat more (\cref{tab:equal-cost}). B2 costs $20\%$ less than A and has a lower mean and median error; C2 costs $1.4\times$
as much, and reduces the mean error from $1.66\%$ to $1.37\%$ and the field error from $0.050$ to $0.045$. The differences are small relative to the spread over geometries,
and each arm is a single run, so we read the table as ``LF labels do not hurt'' rather than as a precise ranking.
\Cref{tab:07-pergeom} shows why: the mean of A and C2 is dominated by one geometry (identifier 27), and on the other five the arms
differ by a few hundredths of a percentage point in either direction.
% src: docs/34 §8.2 per-geometry means; the sub-mean without id 27 and the count of geometries are computed from these numbers
Excluding identifier 27, the mean error is $1.11\%$ for A and $1.06\%$ for C2, and C2 is lower on three of the six geometries (24, 27, 29).
Geometry~27 is the worst in every arm ($2.9$--$4.4\%$); we did not investigate the cause. The advantage of C2 is largest there ($4.44\%\to2.93\%$).

\paragraph{The LF-to-HF gap.} The predictions of arm~C2 have a mean error of $1.35\%$ against the LF labels of the same
test meshes and $1.37\%$ against the HF labels, so the model trained on LF data is only $0.02$ percentage points worse on HF targets than on LF targets.
% src: docs/34 §8.2
This is consistent with the label comparison in \cref{sec:multifidelity}: on identical meshes the LF and HF frequencies differ by
$0.004$--$0.047\%$, and the HF mode lies outside the six-mode LF space by only $0.08$--$0.32\%$ in the mass norm (modes 0--3). The model error is 10 to 50 times larger than either.
% src: docs/34 §8.1 ("10-50 times"; the label differences are for the same 8 meshes)

\paragraph{Ritz bound.} In all evaluations of this study every predicted eigenvalue is above the finite-element eigenvalue of the space it was evaluated in,
with the smallest margin $+0.47\%$ (equal-cost study), consistent with \cref{prop:upper-bound}. In the earlier studies of this section the smallest
margins were $+0.006\%$ and $+0.009\%$ (\cref{sec:07-first,sec:07-transfer}).
% src: docs/34 §8.2 (+0.47 %), §4.1 (+0.006 %), §4.2 (+0.009 %)

\paragraph{Fine-tuning and mixed training are inconclusive.} Arm~D is better than C2 in \cref{tab:equal-cost} (mean error $1.37\%\to1.13\%$), but we cannot attribute the gain to the HF labels. The control run (same
starting point, same 200 steps, LF data only) was lost when the container restarted before its test evaluation; on the three validation geometries it reached a frequency error of $0.031$\,GHz and field error $0.046$,
against $0.029$\,GHz and $0.052$ for D, a mixed signal. Two further runs are not part of the comparison: a mixed LF-40 plus HF-8 run with $8\times80$ steps never entered its
decay phase (eight epochs is fewer than the ten-epoch constant phase) and reached $1.95\%$, and a mixed LF-32 plus HF-8 run with the same schedule was lost on restart at epoch~6.
% src: docs/34 §8.2 (invalid and lost arms; D0 validation numbers)
The mixed schedule should be repeated with a corrected schedule and a control at larger scale; we make no claim about the value of fine-tuning or mixed training here.

\subsection{An earlier, smaller study}\label{sec:07-first}

% src: docs/34 §3, §4, §4.1, §4.3
Before the equal-cost experiment, a model with the same architecture was trained on 14 LF geometries for 30 epochs (two threads, about an hour; lr $10^{-3}$, constant for ten epochs then cosine) with the then-current LF meshes, and evaluated on four HF geometries (identifiers 3, 5, 10, 13) that were never part of training.
Its error on three LF test geometries was $2.07\%$ in frequency and $0.182$ in field. On the HF geometries the frequency errors of modes 0--3 were between $+0.63\%$ and $+3.02\%$, all positive, and the field error of modes 0--2 was
$0.026$--$0.072$. The finite-element floor of the order-2 space lies at most $0.033\%$ above the HF labels, so the error is the model's and not the space's. No additional LF-to-HF gap was visible, which is expected here
since LF and HF frequencies agree to the order of $10^{-4}$ on this family (\cref{sec:multifidelity}).

A first fine-tuning test with two HF geometries (8 more epochs, lr $3\times10^{-4}$, started from the LF checkpoint) was controlled by 8 more epochs on LF data alone. On the two held-out HF geometries the mean error went
from $1.83\%$ and $1.76\%$ to $1.50\%$ and $1.50\%$ for the control, against $1.73\%$ and $1.50\%$ with the HF fine-tune; the gain came from the additional training, not from the HF labels.
% src: docs/34 §4.3 (table)
This is the control that is missing for arm~D above, and it suggests, though it does not prove, that the improvement of D is due to the extra steps. The HF information that fine-tuning could add is in the wall-dependent quantities
($\Qz$, surface peaks) whose loss term is switched off in these runs.

\subsection{Transfer to other spaces}\label{sec:07-transfer}

against the finite-element modes of the evaluated space.

The bound holds in all three spaces (smallest margin $+0.009\%$ to the eigenvalue of the evaluated space). Accuracy does not improve: the frequency error is $0.2$--$0.6$ percentage points \emph{worse} than in the native space,
and the field error is unchanged. This is what the construction predicts. A richer space enlarges the set of admissible fields, but the learned basis $\psi_j$ is the same smooth field, so the model error comes from the basis and not from the space; additionally,
the token density (24 quadrature points per tetrahedron at order 3 against 14 at order 2) and the point-volume features differ from training, a small distribution shift.
% src: docs/34 §4.2
Transfer is therefore a statement about validity (the guarantee and the absence of spurious modes survive a change of space), not about accuracy.

\subsection{Cost}\label{sec:07-cost}

% src: docs/34 §4.2, §6, §8.2
At the sizes used here a direct finite-element solve is of the same order of magnitude as the model. In the order-2 native space the operators and the forward pass took $5.1$ and $2.9$\,s on geometries 3 and 5, against $11.5$ and $6.4$\,s for the finite-element
solve of the same space (\cref{tab:07-transfer}); in the equal-cost study (one thread) the operators took $5$\,s and the forward pass $3.3$\,s against $18$\,s for the solve. The model is thus faster by roughly a factor of two on these meshes (a ratio computed from these timings),
with about $2.2$ to $2.7$ at order~3 and about $3.5$ on the refined mesh ($54$\,s against $192$\,s), the largest space tested. At the same time its error is $1$--$2\%$, whereas the finite-element solve is exact up to the discretisation error of its space.
We do not claim a speed advantage at this scale. The potential benefit is at larger spaces and, with a GPU, where the network's share would shrink (not measured), and in combination with an iterative solver started from the model's subspace (not implemented).

\subsection{Engineering}\label{sec:07-engineering}

% src: docs/31 §3.6, §3.7; docs/28 §4
Training on curved high-order meshes is dominated by work that does not depend on the learned parameters. \Cref{tab:07-engineering} lists the reductions we measured, all of which leave the computed quantities unchanged: the training loss, the input tensors and all 76 parameter gradients are
bit-identical to the previous code on both test geometries. (The one exception is a faster torsion-feature solver, whose float32 output was unchanged in all 182\,728 entries on one geometry but differed by at most $6\times10^{-8}$ in 7 of $1.33\times10^{6}$ entries on a 95k-tetrahedron mesh.) Timings are single-thread CPU times on a loaded machine, repeatable to about $1\%$ between runs, wall-clock numbers vary by about $30\%$.

The training-step gain comes from three changes with identical results: the wall features are stored in the data file instead of being recomputed; the sparse-matrix products carry a transpose prepared once per geometry (it removes a sort of $0.5$--$0.7$\,s per matrix in
every backward pass); and the conjugate-gradient projection solve writes its updates into preallocated buffers ($15$--$20\%$ less CG time, same iterate and iteration count). Activation checkpointing in the trunk reduces the peak memory from $9.9$ to $3.8$\,GB at an unchanged step time within
measurement noise on CPU, with identical gradients. The gain grows with the number of tokens and nonzeros, which is why it is $20\%$ on the pillbox and $5.5\%$ on the smaller elliptical cell. Not adopted, after measurement: a per-geometry sparse Cholesky factorisation of $\Kp$
(about $5\%$ of the step), a looser CG tolerance (it changes the gradients), and a smaller operator cache (about 78\,MB against $\sim$1.2\,s per geometry). GPU timing was not measured.
